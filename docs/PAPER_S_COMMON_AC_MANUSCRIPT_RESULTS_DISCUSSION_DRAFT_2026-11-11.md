## Proposed Methods addition: original ReEDS2022 meteorology and consistent AC nameplates

Hourly reference profiles for onshore wind and utility-scale PV were computed from the original ReEDS2022 county-class HDF5 sources and their corresponding source supply-curve available-capacity weights, for Arizona, Colorado, Idaho and Wyoming. The original 2012 dataset provides 8,760 source instants on a publisher-defined fixed-CST (UTC−06:00) reference-year slice. This release's 2012 reference support includes leap-day entries and omits 31 December, so it is **not** a conventionally complete leap-calendar year. The resulting curves are state-level resource-potential-weighted **capacity factors**, not profiles of a documented installed 2012 or projected installed 2030 fleet. Original HDF5, supply-curve MD5, native hour labels, source column alignment and derived state profiles were checked in [source run 42962740548](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/42962740548).

The source wind capacity basis is AC; source PV capacity factors represent grid AC output divided by array DC MW. For a nominal common-AC nameplate experiment, each PV capacity factor was multiplied **once** by the publisher's reference inverter-loading ratio (ILR) of **1.34**, with only a source-decimal-rounding tolerance above unit CF and a clip to unity. Scenario wind and PV weights, and combined renewable nameplate-to-peak ratios, were then defined in common AC MW. The corresponding PV array DC nameplate is 1.34 times its assumed inverter AC MW. A separate original-denominator sensitivity preserves the previous source-basis portfolio convention rather than silently reinterpreting old results as physically identical portfolios.

Original weather curves are labeled **instantaneous at source hour** whereas modeled source demand is provided on an **hour-ending** basis. The primary model uses trapezoidal interpolation between adjacent source instants with an explicitly declared first-hour endpoint approximation, rather than claiming publisher-certified interval-integrated generation. Prior work compared end-instant hold and trapezoid approximations across 36 matched source configurations; this remains a structural uncertainty and must appear in the paper's limits.

The dispatch experiment models four independent, perfectly interconnected *within-state* copperplate systems using projected 2030 demand with the 2012 weather reference. The combined wind+PV AC nameplate is assumed at 0.9, 1.5 or 2.0 times demand peak. Storage is a cyclic-hourly LP with no storage and 4/8-hour storage sensitivity, nominal discharge/charge power 10% or 25% of demand peak, illustrative 90% round-trip efficiency, renewable marginal cost $5/MWh and firm energy marginal cost $90/MWh. A subsequent 108-case comparison spans efficiencies 80/90/95% and firm marginal costs $60/90/120 per MWh with AC nameplate ratio 1.5, wind AC nameplate share 50%, and power ratio 0.25. The LP neither optimizes capital expansion nor includes generator commitment, grid flows, transmission costs, capacity adequacy, reserves, degradation, investment financing or battery CAPEX.

Define the annual *illustrative avoided variable operating objective* as `S = C_0 - C_D`, where `C_D` is the optimal variable operating objective with storage duration D. For duration expansion 4h→8h at fixed storage power P, define an operating-value-only incremental energy annuity hurdle `H_E = [C_4 - C_8] / [E_8-E_4]` ($/kWh-year). This is an upper bound on supportable incremental annualized energy cost **conditional on zero other incremental expenses and no additional revenue streams**. It is neither net present value nor a quoted battery investment cost.

## Proposed Results 3.1: capacity convention matters for renewable availability and storage dispatch

The common-AC resource formulation changed modeled renewable supply relative to an earlier heterogeneous source-denominator formulation. In matched illustrative scenarios for 2030 demand, assumed renewable AC nameplate equal to 1.5 times peak load, wind nominal share 50%, 4h battery and storage power 25% of peak, the avoided annual variable operating objective changed from $218.24 million to $387.85 million (AZ), $64.08 million to $162.25 million (CO), $21.14 million to $55.49 million (ID), and $11.61 million to $27.59 million (WY). The corrected scenario's power-only annuity upper bounds are, respectively, $79.95, $56.66, $42.65, and $35.96 per kW-year. All LP runs were feasible/optimal and checked for hourly energy balance [common-AC run 43058074434](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43058074434).

These changes **cannot be described as a numerical correction of the same physical portfolio**: at the same nominal scenario ratio, an AC-denominated PV inverter fleet implies 1.34 times the PV array DC capacity compared with the source-denominator experiment. Thus this result quantifies the importance of portfolio-definition consistency, rather than observational bias in operating savings or an empirical correction factor.

The broader 0.9/1.5/2.0 source-denominator nameplate sensitivity demonstrated that higher assumed renewable capacity coincided with more potential curtailment and greater modeled storage benefits, with Arizona's no-storage curtailment rising from 1.173% to 21.162%, and Wyoming from 0.0026% to 8.037% in one fixed 2030 reference case [original 144 LP run 42966023512](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/42966023512). Those percentages are **source-denominator diagnostic outcomes, not common-AC main-case estimates**. The corrected common-AC 3×3 ratio/wind-share study is shown instead in the eight new candidate panels [AC figure run 43059602600](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43059602600). Do not present the original curtailment numbers alongside corrected AC nameplate cost values as if they belonged to one common LP case.

## Proposed Results 3.2: energy-duration expansion has a low but scenario-dependent marginal hurdle

At 2030 demand, 2012 meteorology, combined hypothetical common AC nameplate 1.5 times peak, wind AC share 50%, and storage power 25% of peak, moving from 4h to 8h increased avoided annual variable operating costs by $212.59 million in AZ, $71.25 million in CO, $21.52 million in ID and $10.87 million in WY. Dividing these increments by the **additional energy storage capacity** yielded modeled energy-only annualized hurdle thresholds of **$10.9554**, **$6.2207**, **$4.1348**, and **$3.5406** per kWh-year, respectively [144 AC/legacy incremental cases, run 43058722043](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43058722043).

For an explicitly illustrative annualized *additional energy capacity* cost of $10/kWh-year, only the Arizona central case clears this partial-cost screen, by $0.9554/kWh-year **before any other incremental costs**. These quantities measure annual marginal operating benefit from one weather-year realization and given model prices. They should not be interpreted as actual profitable deployment, physical reliability improvement, or observed future market earnings.

## Proposed Results 3.3: cost–efficiency sensitivity overturns a categorical 8-hour recommendation

Under a prespecified 108-LP sensitivity (four states × three battery round-trip efficiencies × three firm generation marginal costs × 0/4/8h), the Arizona 4→8h marginal energy annuity ceiling ranged from **$6.1982 to $15.6492/kWh-year**. Its baseline at 90% round-trip efficiency and firm marginal cost $90/MWh was reproduced at **$10.9554/kWh-year**; reducing round-trip efficiency to 80% at the same firm cost reduced the hurdle to **$9.6576/kWh-year**, while reducing firm generation marginal cost to $60/MWh at 90% efficiency lowered it to **$7.0634/kWh-year**. Increasing firm generation cost to $120/MWh at 90% efficiency raised it to **$14.8475/kWh-year** [108 LP source run 43060677031](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43060677031).

Against the illustrative $10/kWh-year *energy-cost-only* hurdle, exactly **five of nine** Arizona parameter combinations cleared it. By comparison, the maxima among the nine conditions were **$8.9986** for Colorado, **$5.9804** for Idaho, and **$5.1273/kWh-year** for Wyoming, so **none of their nine parameter combinations** passed that hypothetical marginal cost screen. The 5/9 and 0/9 values are *counts of deterministic factorial conditions*, not probabilities or uncertainty intervals. All 108 LPs and the 36 unique duration-matched triplets passed solver and energy-balance checks; zero-storage objective invariance and Arizona original-anchor replication were separately enforced. [Efficiency–cost figure run 43061658578](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43061658578) provides eight supporting state heatmaps (absolute hurdle and hurdle-minus-$10 threshold).

## Proposed Discussion 4.1: unit conventions, nonlinearity and economic threshold

The primary finding is not that 8-hour storage is inherently profitable in Arizona or uneconomic elsewhere. Rather, a *common-capacity-basis, source-derived-weather* optimization shows how marginal storage operating value depends on the joint definition of renewable nameplate, penetration, wind/PV mix, storage duration and power, battery efficiency and the cost of dispatchable replacement electricity. A portfolio described as 50% wind and 50% solar is not reproducible unless the AC/DC denominator is explicit. Interpreting a total operating saving without the additional power and energy dimensions also obscures whether an expansion's marginal benefit can cover its extra cost. With the $10/kWh-year illustrative incremental energy assumption, even the state with the highest modeled marginal benefit is sensitive to operating assumptions.

## Proposed Discussion 4.2: scientific limitations and admission gates

First, the renewable CF are *available-resource-weighted*, not the geographic layout or mix of any installed or modeled future fleet. Second, source CF point observations have no independently certified equivalence to hour-integrated renewable energy, despite fixed-CST native labels and separately documented hour-ending demand. Third, the baseline requires unrealistic single-state copperplate dispatch, no transmission congestion, no unit commitment, and no reserves/capacity adequacy. Fourth, battery CAPEX, cycle degradation, fixed O&M, replacement, financial assumptions and non-energy revenues are absent. Fifth, this study uses **one source weather reference year, 2012**, not a climate or weather-year ensemble. Sixth, no independent statistically sampled data or inferential confidence interval is offered. Seventh, current ReEDS2022 weather and demand timestamp semantics must not be conflated with Paper T's still-blocked **EFS2020↔ReEDS2022** cross-release clock admission.

Consequently, reported dollar values should be labelled *hypothetical 2030 scenario operating objective differences under 2012 reference weather*, not future regional electricity bill savings, battery procurement recommendations, empirical hourly dispatch effects, or net project value. A mainline publication claim requires cross-year physical-weather and installed-fleet checks, additional source-interval integration sensitivity on the **common-AC** model, and scientific review of cost/market and network simplifications. The common-AC experiment does not cure these limitations.

## Proposed Results 3.4: direct common-AC source-hour approximation sensitivity (new verified evidence)

A dedicated **216-LP same-source sensitivity** varied the interpretation of original ReEDS2022 instantaneous CFs while holding common-AC nameplate, projected 2030 demand, 2012 source weather, battery specifications and illustration cost assumptions fixed. Under end-instant hold, an observed source CF at hour t was interpreted directly as hourly availability; under trapezoidal approximation, previous and current source observations were averaged, with a noncyclic first-hour endpoint fallback. None of these interpolations is certified as the actual prior-hour resource-energy integral.

All 216 models were optimal with conservation checks. In addition, all **108** trapezoidal cases reproduced original archived common-AC source-model objectives to within **$0.005** of their frozen reference [216-LP verified run 43080107709](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43080107709). Among 36 matched nameplate penetration/wind-mix scenarios, **no** comparison changed the binary $10/kWh-year energy-annuity screening result for 4h→8h duration expansion. However, the absolute annuity hurdle changed by up to **$1.0505/kWh-year**. In the 1.5× peak AC penetration and 50% wind base scenario, Arizona's incremental 4→8h energy-only hurdle was **$11.9003** under end-instant hold and **$10.9554/kWh-year** under trapezoidal approximation, with Colorado **$6.8536 vs $6.2207**, Idaho **$4.4835 vs $4.1348**, and Wyoming **$3.7544 vs $3.5406/kWh-year**, respectively.

The lack of a binary screening decision flip is *conditional stability* in this chosen three-ratio/three-mix/four-state design, not confirmation of the instantaneous-to-hour-ending energy mapping, nor a guarantee at other price/efficiency levels. It should be presented alongside the separate η × firm-cost sensitivity, which demonstrates actual binary decision flips for Arizona when those parameters vary.
