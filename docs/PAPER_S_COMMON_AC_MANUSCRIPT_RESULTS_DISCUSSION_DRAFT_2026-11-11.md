## Proposed Methods addition: original ReEDS2022 meteorology and consistent AC nameplates

Hourly reference profiles for onshore wind and utility-scale PV were computed from the original ReEDS2022 county-class HDF5 sources and their corresponding source supply-curve available-capacity weights, for Arizona, Colorado, Idaho and Wyoming. The original 2012 dataset provides 8,760 source instants on a publisher-defined fixed-CST (UTC−06:00) reference-year slice. This release's 2012 reference support includes leap-day entries and omits 31 December, so it is **not** a conventionally complete leap-calendar year. The resulting curves are state-level resource-potential-weighted **capacity factors**, not profiles of a documented installed 2012 or projected installed 2030 fleet. Original HDF5, supply-curve MD5, native hour labels, source column alignment and derived state profiles were checked in [source run 42962740548](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/42962740548).

The source wind capacity basis is AC; source PV capacity factors represent grid AC output divided by array DC MW. For a nominal common-AC nameplate experiment, each PV capacity factor was multiplied **once** by the publisher's reference inverter-loading ratio (ILR) of **1.34**, with only a source-decimal-rounding tolerance above unit CF and a clip to unity. Scenario wind and PV weights, and combined renewable nameplate-to-peak ratios, were then defined in common AC MW. The corresponding PV array DC nameplate is 1.34 times its assumed inverter AC MW. A separate original-denominator sensitivity preserves the previous source-basis portfolio convention rather than silently reinterpreting old results as physically identical portfolios.

Original weather curves are labeled **instantaneous at source hour** whereas modeled source demand is provided on an **hour-ending** basis. The primary model uses trapezoidal interpolation between adjacent source instants with an explicitly declared first-hour endpoint approximation, rather than claiming publisher-certified interval-integrated generation. Prior work compared end-instant hold and trapezoid approximations across 36 matched source configurations; this remains a structural uncertainty and must appear in the paper's limits.

The dispatch experiment models four independent, perfectly interconnected *within-state* copperplate systems using projected 2030 demand with the 2012 weather reference. The combined wind+PV AC nameplate is assumed at 0.9, 1.5 or 2.0 times demand peak. Storage is a cyclic-hourly LP with no storage and 4/8-hour storage sensitivity, nominal discharge/charge power 10% or 25% of demand peak, illustrative 90% round-trip efficiency, renewable marginal cost $5/MWh and firm energy marginal cost $90/MWh. A subsequent 108-case comparison spans efficiencies 80/90/95% and firm marginal costs $60/90/120 per MWh with AC nameplate ratio 1.5, wind AC nameplate share 50%, and power ratio 0.25. The LP neither optimizes capital expansion nor includes generator commitment, grid flows, transmission costs, capacity adequacy, reserves, degradation, investment financing or battery CAPEX.

Define the annual *illustrative avoided variable operating objective* as `S = C_0 - C_D`, where `C_D` is the optimal variable operating objective with storage duration D. For duration expansion 4h→8h at fixed storage power P, define an operating-value-only incremental energy annuity hurdle `H_E = [C_4 - C_8] / [E_8-E_4]` ($/kWh-year). This is an upper bound on supportable incremental annualized energy cost **conditional on zero other incremental expenses and no additional revenue streams**. It is neither net present value nor a quoted battery investment cost.

## Proposed Results 3.1: capacity convention matters for renewable availability and storage dispatch

The common-AC resource formulation changed modeled renewable supply relative to an earlier heterogeneous source-denominator formulation. In matched illustrative scenarios for 2030 demand, assumed renewable AC nameplate equal to 1.5 times peak load, wind nominal share 50%, 4h battery and storage power 25% of peak, the avoided annual variable operating objective changed from $218.24 million to $387.85 million (AZ), $64.08 million to $162.25 million (CO), $21.14 million to $55.49 million (ID), and $11.61 million to $27.59 million (WY). The corrected scenario's power-only annuity upper bounds are, respectively, $79.95, $56.66, $42.65, and $35.96 per kW-year. All LP runs were feasible/optimal and checked for hourly energy balance [common-AC run 43058074434](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43058074434).

These changes **cannot be described as a numerical correction of the same physical portfolio**: at the same nominal scenario ratio, an AC-denominated PV inverter fleet implies 1.34 times the PV array DC capacity compared with the source-denominator experiment. Thus this result quantifies the importance of portfolio-definition consistency, rather than observational bias in operating savings or an empirical correction factor.

The broader 0.9/1.5/2.0 source-denominator nameplate sensitivity demonstrated that higher assumed renewable capacity coincided with more potential curtailment and greater modeled storage benefits, with Arizona's no-storage curtailment rising from 1.173% to 21.162%, and Wyoming from 0.0026% to 8.037% in one fixed 2030 reference case [original 144 LP run 42966023512](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/42966023512). Those percentages are **source-denominator diagnostic outcomes, not common-AC main-case estimates**. The corrected common-AC 3×3 ratio/wind-share study is shown instead in the eight new candidate panels [AC figure run 43059602600](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43059602600). Do not present the original curtailment numbers alongside corrected AC nameplate cost values as if they belonged to one common LP case.

## Proposed Results 3.2: energy-duration expansion has a low but scenario-dependent marginal hurdle

At 2030 demand, 2012 meteorology, combined hypothetical common AC nameplate 1.5 times peak, wind AC share 50%, and storage power 25% of peak, moving from 4h to 8h increased avoided annual variable operating costs by $212.59 million in AZ, $71.25 million in CO, $21.52 million in ID and $10.87 million in WY. Dividing these increments by the **additional energy storage capacity** yielded modeled energy-only annualized hurdle thresholds of **$10.9554**, **$6.2207**, **$4.1348**, and **$3.5406** per kWh-year, respectively [144 AC/legacy incremental cases, run 43058722043](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43058722043).

For an explicitly illustrative annualized *additional energy capacity* cost of $10/kWh-year, only the Arizona central case clears this partial-cost screen, by $0.9554/kWh-year **before any other incremental costs**. These quantities measure annual marginal operating benefit from one weather-year realization and given model prices. They should not be interpreted as actual profitable deployment, physical reliability improvement, or observed future market earnings.

## Proposed Results 3.3: cost–efficiency sensitivity overturns a categorical 8-hour recommendation

Under a prespecified 108-LP sensitivity (four states × three battery round-trip efficiencies × three firm generation marginal costs × 0/4/8h), the Arizona 4→8h marginal energy annuity ceiling ranged from **$6.1982 to $15.6492/kWh-year**. Its baseline at 90% round-trip efficiency and firm marginal cost $90/MWh was reproduced at **$10.9554/kWh-year**; reducing round-trip efficiency to 80% at the same firm cost reduced the hurdle to **$9.6576/kWh-year**, while reducing firm generation marginal cost to $60/MWh at 90% efficiency lowered it to **$7.0634/kWh-year**. Increasing firm generation cost to $120/MWh at 90% efficiency raised it to **$14.8475/kWh-year** [108 LP source run 43060677031](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43060677031).

Against the illustrative $10/kWh-year *energy-cost-only* hurdle, exactly **five of nine** Arizona parameter combinations cleared it. By comparison, the maxima among the nine conditions were **$8.9986** for Colorado, **$5.9804** for Idaho, and **$5.1273/kWh-year** for Wyoming, so **none of their nine parameter combinations** passed that hypothetical marginal cost screen. The 5/9 and 0/9 values are *counts of deterministic factorial conditions*, not probabilities or uncertainty intervals. All 108 LPs and the 36 unique duration-matched triplets passed solver and energy-balance checks; zero-storage objective invariance and Arizona original-anchor replication were separately enforced. [Efficiency–cost figure run 43061658578](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43061658578) provides eight supporting state heatmaps (absolute hurdle and hurdle-minus-$10 threshold).

## Proposed Discussion 4.1: unit conventions, nonlinearity and economic threshold

The primary finding is not that 8-hour storage is inherently profitable in Arizona or uneconomic elsewhere. Rather, a *common-capacity-basis, source-derived-weather* optimization shows how marginal storage operating value depends on the joint definition of renewable nameplate, penetration, wind/PV mix, storage duration and power, battery efficiency and the cost of dispatchable replacement electricity. A portfolio described as 50% wind and 50% solar is not reproducible unless the AC/DC denominator is explicit. Interpreting a total operating saving without the additional power and energy dimensions also obscures whether an expansion's marginal benefit can cover its extra cost. With the $10/kWh-year illustrative incremental energy assumption, even the state with the highest modeled marginal benefit is sensitive to operating assumptions.

## Proposed Discussion 4.2: scientific limitations and admission gates

First, the renewable CF are *available-resource-weighted*, not the geographic layout or mix of any installed or modeled future fleet. Second, source CF point observations have no independently certified equivalence to hour-integrated renewable energy, despite fixed-CST native labels and separately documented hour-ending demand. Third, the baseline requires unrealistic single-state copperplate dispatch, no transmission congestion, no unit commitment, and no reserves/capacity adequacy. Fourth, battery CAPEX, cycle degradation, fixed O&M, replacement, financial assumptions and non-energy revenues are absent. Fifth, this study uses **one source weather reference year, 2012**, not a climate or weather-year ensemble. Sixth, no independent statistically sampled data or inferential confidence interval is offered. Seventh, current ReEDS2022 weather and demand timestamp semantics must not be conflated with Paper T's still-blocked **EFS2020↔ReEDS2022** cross-release clock admission.

Consequently, reported dollar values should be labelled *hypothetical 2030 scenario operating objective differences under 2012 reference weather*, not future regional electricity bill savings, battery procurement recommendations, empirical hourly dispatch effects, or net project value. A mainline publication claim requires cross-year physical-weather and installed-fleet checks, additional source-interval integration sensitivity on the **common-AC** model, and scientific review of cost/market and network simplifications. The common-AC experiment does not cure these limitations.

## Proposed Results 3.4: direct common-AC source-hour approximation sensitivity (new verified evidence)

A dedicated **216-LP same-source sensitivity** varied the interpretation of original ReEDS2022 instantaneous CFs while holding common-AC nameplate, projected 2030 demand, 2012 source weather, battery specifications and illustration cost assumptions fixed. Under end-instant hold, an observed source CF at hour t was interpreted directly as hourly availability; under trapezoidal approximation, previous and current source observations were averaged, with a noncyclic first-hour endpoint fallback. None of these interpolations is certified as the actual prior-hour resource-energy integral.

All 216 models were optimal with conservation checks. In addition, all **108** trapezoidal cases reproduced original archived common-AC source-model objectives to within **$0.005** of their frozen reference [216-LP verified run 43080107709](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43080107709). Among 36 matched nameplate penetration/wind-mix scenarios, **no** comparison changed the binary $10/kWh-year energy-annuity screening result for 4h→8h duration expansion. However, the absolute annuity hurdle changed by up to **$1.0505/kWh-year**. In the 1.5× peak AC penetration and 50% wind base scenario, Arizona's incremental 4→8h energy-only hurdle was **$11.9003** under end-instant hold and **$10.9554/kWh-year** under trapezoidal approximation, with Colorado **$6.8536 vs $6.2207**, Idaho **$4.4835 vs $4.1348**, and Wyoming **$3.7544 vs $3.5406/kWh-year**, respectively.

The lack of a binary screening decision flip is *conditional stability* in this chosen three-ratio/three-mix/four-state design, not confirmation of the instantaneous-to-hour-ending energy mapping, nor a guarantee at other price/efficiency levels. It should be presented alongside the separate η × firm-cost sensitivity, which demonstrates actual binary decision flips for Arizona when those parameters vary.

## Proposed Results 3.5: resource-potential weighting is spatially concentrated (authenticated county-supply audit)

A distinct source-schema-and-MD5-verified spatial audit reconstructed the **county shares of publisher-declared developable resource potential**, without claiming installed electricity generation. In the four study states, positive-potential county counts were 15 (AZ), 61–63 (CO), 44 (ID), and 23 (WY), depending on technology. The effective county count, calculated as the reciprocal of the sum of squared county resource-potential shares, ranged from **8.70 to 36.35** across the eight wind/PV state-technology pairs. Arizona utility PV showed the strongest five-county concentration: **66.33%** of its developable PV MW were located in the five largest county shares, giving an effective count of **8.70** despite **15** positive-potential counties. Colorado PV had **63** source-potential counties but an effective count of **27.30**. The total variation distance between source potential-weighted and a purely hypothetical equal-positive-county weighting was **0.330** in AZ PV and **0.441** in CO PV. These values characterize *spatial weights*, not a realized shift in operating cost or capacity factor; a county-level CF replay is required to establish resulting model sensitivity [source concentration audit 43120911728](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43120911728).

## Proposed Discussion 4.3: spatial representativeness of a potential-weighted profile

The concentration analysis provides direct evidence that state CF profiles are sensitive to the geographical distribution of ReEDS *developable resource potential*, and that an unweighted county average is not the same population of source locations. Neither potential weights nor an equal-county counterfactual can be interpreted as a measured 2030 generating fleet. A more credible independent benchmark is the U.S. Energy Information Administration's **2025 EIA-860** plant- and generator-level file, which records existing generator technology, nameplate capacity, and plant county/state location. The present analysis has not yet matched the official plant/gen tables into a verified EIA fleet county FIPS table, and 2025 observed capacity is itself not a validated 2030 portfolio. Any prospective plant-weighted model would need independent status and technology filters, generator-to-plant ID joins, validated five-digit county identifiers, and explicit AC/DC denominator consistency. The verified ReEDS spatial concentration values should therefore be treated as a methodological limit and motivation for independent siting sensitivity, **not as calibration evidence that the LP already represents current or future power plants**.


## Proposed Results 3.6: independent EIA 2025 operating-fleet siting check (verified new evidence)

A separate independent external-data check compared original ReEDS2022 county developable wind/PV resource-potential fractions with the geographic allocation of **operating 2025 EIA-860 plant-generator AC nameplate** within the same four study states. The original 2025 EIA workbook `Operable` was filtered to `Status=OP` and technology-consistent wind WT/WND or photovoltaic PV/SUN, mapped to the `Plant` worksheet by plant ID, and independently assigned official Census county FIPS using the exact state and county names. All eight technology-by-state nameplate groups achieved **100% mapped nameplate coverage**, without fuzzy county matches. For each technology, the source ReEDS resource-potential shares were normalized within their own original capacity denominator (wind AC or PV DC), and compared with observed EIA operating generator AC-nameplate **shares** over the union of county areas.

The county-capacity allocation differed substantially, with **total variation distances of 0.6036–0.8620** for the eight comparisons. Arizona's operating photovoltaic nameplate represented an effective **3.68 counties**, versus **8.70** when weighted by original ReEDS developable PV potential; Idaho wind represented **4.82 observed effective counties versus 27.82 potential-weighted counties**. In Wyoming, operating 2025 photovoltaic generators were reported in only **two counties** (242 MW AC), versus **23** counties with positive ReEDS resource potential; the resulting county weight total variation was **0.8620**. Other cases were AZ wind 0.6036, CO wind 0.7338, CO PV 0.6711, ID PV 0.8139 and WY wind 0.6933. Full eight-row original-source audit and county FIPS comparison are verified in [2025 EIA versus ReEDS county weight analysis 43124063185](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43124063185).

These measured *capacity-siting distribution* discrepancies do not imply an observed change in hourly capacity factor or storage operating costs. The original ReEDS resource-class/county time series must be independently reaggregated with EIA 2025 county capacity fractions, under explicit within-county class assumptions, before a paired LP sensitivity can quantify that impact.

## Proposed Discussion 4.4: limits of resource-potential weighted state renewable profiles

The comparison with 2025 in-operation EIA generator geography demonstrates why a state profile aggregated by *developable resource potential* should not be interpreted as the weather-weighted production series of actual generating units. For all eight state-technology comparisons, the 2025 installed generator county capacity weights were materially different from the assumed available-resource-potential weights, and existing generator capacity was substantially more spatially concentrated. This empirical siting evidence increases the relevance of a spatial reweighting experiment, but it does not provide installed generator technology class, vintage, location within a county, measured hourly energy or a projected 2030 siting map. Even an LP recomputed using 2025 EIA county fractions with 2012 ReEDS source weather and 2030 demand would remain a **cross-year spatial counterfactual**, not a validated 2030 generation forecast or net battery investment model.

## Proposed Results 3.7: 2025 observed county siting alters 4-to-8-hour storage value (verified source-native LP)

We propagated the independently audited **2025 EIA-860 operating-generator county capacity shares** through the original **2012 ReEDS county × resource-class instantaneous wind and utility-PV HDF5** to create separate 8760-index state reference profiles, retaining the original ReEDS resource-potential proportions **within each county**. The alternative county assignment therefore perturbs only geographic installed-capacity shares rather than technology vintage, resource class, power rating or nominal state AC nameplate. Independent frozen source-potential state CF were reproduced at floating-point accuracy (all eight state/tech maximum errors approximately 3–7×10⁻¹⁶), and original 12 common-AC reference LP objective values reproduced within the prespecified $0.10 cost tolerance. All **24 matched scenario LPs** (four states × source-potential or EIA 2025 county weighting × 0h/4h/8h storage) solved optimally with energy-balance validation [complete source-native run 43159121663](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43159121663).

In the fixed 2030 state demand and 2012 ReEDS reference weather model, with hypothetical renewable AC nameplate 1.5× peak demand, wind 50% AC, battery power 25% peak, round-trip efficiency 90%, renewable $5/MWh and firm $90/MWh variable marginal costs, reassigning the geographical county fractions **reduced Arizona's marginal 4h-to-8h annual energy-annuity operating-value ceiling from $10.9554 to $9.8683/kWh-year**. The geographic perturbation **reversed** the qualitative screening judgment against the deliberately illustrative $10/kWh-year *incremental energy-capacity-only* annualized cost. Colorado decreased from $6.2207 to $5.9433, whereas Idaho increased from $4.1348 to $5.7899 and Wyoming from $3.5406 to $5.9776/kWh-year. The latter three states did not cross the illustrative $10/kWh-year screen under either geographic weighting.

The four-hour battery's modeled annual variable-operating savings were $387.85M vs $383.94M in Arizona, $162.25M vs $172.42M in Colorado, $55.49M vs $67.90M in Idaho and $27.59M vs $39.88M in Wyoming (source-potential vs EIA county weighting, respectively). The more material marginal-duration effect in Arizona is that the **incremental 4h-to-8h avoided variable cost** falls from $212.59M to $191.49M with 2025 observed county siting fractions. These are **reference weather-year stylized model savings**, not 2025 observed generator earnings or 2030 actual grid avoided costs.

## Proposed Discussion 4.5: geographic siting is another independent axis of structural uncertainty

The spatial sensitivity supplies a direct demonstration that geographic assumptions can change not only available county potential shares (previous results, TV 0.604–0.862), but also the annual timing of aggregated renewable resource availability and the LP's marginal storage-operating value. Crucially, **the Arizona $10/kWh-year binary result flips with the external EIA county siting perturbation**, whereas the earlier same-source hour-end-hold versus numerical trapezoid comparison showed zero flips in its 36 prespecified state/mix/capacity cases. This apparent difference is not a ranking of universally important uncertainties: each experiment varies different factors and has different scope. Combined, the economic threshold is conditional on generation cost, roundtrip efficiency, resource siting, source-hour availability approximation, resource mix and installed AC nameplate.

This is a 2025-siting-weighted **2012 source-weather × projected 2030 demand counterfactual** rather than an empirical model of actual 2025 production or a defensible forecast of the exact 2030 fleet. Only county placement fractions are constrained by EIA; conditional within-county renewable class composition is still inherited from the ReEDS source available-resource potential, and meteorology and source hour-to-energy integration remain uncalibrated. Thus the counterfactual can reject *siting-invariant interpretations* of the existing hypothetical LP, but cannot validate a real installed 2030 dispatch outcome or project investment NPV.

## Proposed Results 3.8: four native weather references show a year-dependent siting effect (verified)

To test whether the observed 2012 siting-sensitive storage-operating-value screen was general across alternative meteorological realizations, we extended the same county-class source reaggregation and 2030 source-load LP to independent original ReEDS historical weather years **2010, 2011, 2012 and 2013**. The validated original wind/PV HDF5 contains 15 weather-source years **2007–2013 and 2016–2023**, not a continuous 2007–2021 record; each source technology's native clock and exact 8760-point annual time support were independently source-MD5 authenticated. In leap source years, the publisher's native 8760-point reference included February 29 but omitted December 31. For nonleap weather-year comparators, all original source months/days/hours were mapped explicitly to the 2012 demand-support time labels; the absent 24 February 29 samples were *numerically interpolated* from same-hour February 28 and March 1 samples and the 24 native December 31 hours excluded. The resulting 64 geography/technology/state/year calendar diagnostics retain a quantitative annual CF-hour substitution audit. This is a transparent numerical sensitivity mapping, not independently observed leap-day meteorology, physically certified hourly energy, or a contemporaneous weather-load pair.

All **96** prespecified LPs (four weather years × four states × original publisher resource potential or observed EIA 2025 operating-generator county weights × 0h/4h/8h duration) solved optimally. The full **24** original 2012 comparison LP objectives were reproduced, with maximum source numerical deviation **$0.004863**, below the prespecified $0.10 objective tolerance. Exact source/weather-year and optimization artifacts: [verified workflow 43281182390](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43281182390); original 15-year wind/PV clocks [verified 43281182402](https://github.com/ys30/Energy-Transition-Analysis-PyPSA/actions/runs/43281182402).

For Arizona, the potential- versus EIA-2025-county-weighted marginal operating-value-only energy annuity was **$11.068 vs $10.727/kWh-year (2010), $12.017 vs $11.421 (2011), $10.955 vs $9.868 (2012), and $12.821 vs $12.842 (2013)**. The deliberately illustrative $10/kWh-year additional energy-cost screen therefore reversed in **2012 alone**; both geographical treatments exceeded $10 in the other three source weather years. Across all four states and four weather years, there was precisely **one threshold flip in 16 paired comparisons**, not a statistical estimate of any future flip probability. For the other three states, neither geography attained the hypothetical $10/kWh-year marginal ceiling under any of these four weather years. Spatial weighting altered the value in each comparison, but the magnitude and sometimes the direction varied with historical source weather. ## Proposed Discussion 4.6: avoid generalizing the 2012 Arizona binary screen

The independently replicated four-weather-year panel qualifies, rather than removes, the single-2012 spatial-siting conclusion. Original source HDF5 county resources and authenticated EIA 2025 operating county capacity shares demonstrate that geographic siting assumptions can materially change hypothetical marginal storage operating value. **But the Arizona reversal of an illustrative $10/kWh-year screening threshold was confined to the 2012 reference among the four weather years evaluated**. This is a model-structural interaction between weather realization and geographical source-siting fractions, not a validated climate-change effect or a demonstrated future market feasibility reversal. An editor should foreground the continuous magnitude of the marginal value changes and report the threshold only as an explicitly conditional screening example. Treating 2012 as a representative or forecast weather realization would overstate the evidence, and a count of one reversal among four weather years is not a future probability. Spatial and meteorological variability do not independently resolve source point-CF versus hourly-integrated-generation semantics, 2025-to-2030 facility vintage/projected siting, regional grid constraints, or project-level CAPEX/NPV.
